This is an illustrative example to give an intuitive understanding of how lenders estimate Expected Credit Loss.

A lender has 5,000 unsecured personal loans on its books. Management asks one question:

“How much money should we expect to lose on this book over the next 12 months?”

To answer it we need three numbers for every loan.

PD
Probability of Default

How likely is this borrower to default in the next 12 months?

From the past behaviour of similar loans

EAD
Exposure at Default

If they default, how much will they owe us?

From the loan balance

LGD
Loss Given Default

Of that amount, what share will we never recover?

From past recovery experience

Expected Loss=PD×LGD×EAD

Part A

How many loans defaulted, and what is the default rate?

Every loan in PL_Accounts carries a flag, default_12m: 1 if the borrower reached 90+ days past due (our definition of default) within 12 months of 31-Mar-2025, 0 if not.

Default rate=Number of loans that defaultedTotal number of loans

Part B

How is the default rate distributed across variables?

We show this for a small set of variables: DPD bucket, bureau score band and city tier. The same check can be run on any other variable, such as FOIR or employment type. For FOIR we compare the average for defaulters and non-defaulters.

Bureau scores are grouped into four bands: below 650, 650–699, 700–749 and 750 or more.

Part C

Segmenting the book: a two-way table of default rates

In practice, lenders often use a multivariate segmentation built from a few relevant and intuitive variables, chosen because they rank-order default rates: as you move from one segment to the next, the default rate rises steadily.

Here we choose DPD bucket and bureau score band. We leave out city tier because it does not rank-order default rates – Part B showed the rate is the same in every tier.

4 score bands × 4 DPD buckets give 16 segments. The default rate of a segment becomes the PD of every loan in it.

Part D

Merging the small segments

For loans that are already late (1-30, 31-60, 61-89 DPD) we keep only two score bands, below 700 and 700+, instead of four. Loans paying on time keep all four bands because there are plenty of them.

16 segments become 10 pools. Each loan then takes its pool’s default rate as its pooled PD.

Part E

A PD for every individual loan: logistic regression

Pools give every loan in a segment the same PD. A logistic regression gives each loan its own PD, and it can use more information than a two-way table – here also FOIR and employment type. City tier is left out because it does not matter.

What the model does, in plain words: it looks at all 5,000 loans together and finds a weight for each characteristic that best separates the loans that defaulted from those that did not. Adding up weight × characteristic gives a score, z. The formula below turns any score into a probability between 0 and 1.

PD=11 + e−zwherez = b0 + b1x1 + b2x2 + …

The model reports each weight as an odds ratio: how many times the odds of default are multiplied when that characteristic goes up by one unit. Above 1 raises the odds; below 1 lowers them.

Three things to know before reading the results

  1. DPD bucket becomes yes/no columns

    DPD bucket is one column with four categories. The model turns it into three 0/1 columns: 1-30, 31-60 and 61-89 DPD. 0 DPD (paying on time) is the baseline and gets no row of its own; each bucket’s odds ratio says how many times higher the odds are compared with a borrower paying on time.

  2. Score and FOIR are scaled to readable steps

    One bureau-score point is too small to mean anything, so a unit is 50 points. One unit of FOIR would be 100% of income, so a unit is 10 percentage points. The odds ratios are read per 50 points of score and per 10 points of FOIR.

  3. Self-employed is a yes/no

    It is 1 for self-employed and 0 for salaried. Its odds ratio compares a self-employed borrower with an otherwise identical salaried one.

Part F

Pooled PD vs individual PD

We now have two PDs for every loan: the pooled PD from Part D, which every loan in a pool shares, and the model PD from Part E, which is the loan’s own. Ten loans, side by side:

Part G

Turning PD into action

A PD is a probability, not a yes/no. To act on it, the lender sets a threshold. In this illustrative case we take the threshold as 20%: a loan with PD of 20% or more is predicted to default, and a loan below 20% is predicted not to.

We then compare the prediction with what actually happened. The 2 × 2 table that does this is called a confusion matrix.

Part H

Exposure at Default (EAD)

For a personal loan this is the simple part. It is an EMI loan with no undrawn limit, so if the borrower defaults, what they owe is essentially the outstanding balance.

Credit cards are different: a borrower can draw more of the limit on the way to default. We will see that separately.

EAD=Outstanding balance on 31-Mar-2025

Part I

Loss Given Default (LGD)

When a loan defaults, the lender does not lose everything. Collections, settlements and legal action bring some money back – but slowly, over months and years. LGD is the share of the balance at default that is never recovered.

We cannot measure this on today’s book: loans that defaulted during the last year are still being chased. So lenders look at older defaults whose recoveries have played out. PL_Recovery_History follows 960 loans that defaulted between Apr-2021 and Mar-2023, month by month for three years after default.

I1A first look at the recovery history

A few loans and their first months of recoveries.

I2The recovery curve

Add up all recoveries month by month, divide by the total balance at default, and accumulate. The result is the cumulative recovery rate: by month m after default, what share of the defaulted money has come back?

I3From the curve to LGD

LGD=1 − recovery rate at the point where the curve flattens

We check the curve at 12, 18, 24 and 30 months. The curve has flattened at the first of these after which the next six months add less than 1% of the balance.

I4Does every year recover the same?

A separate recovery curve for each quarter in which loans defaulted.

I5(Optional) The time value of recoveries

A rupee recovered two years after default is worth less than a rupee on the day of default. Some lenders – and accounting standards for provisioning – adjust for this by discounting each month’s recovery. Here we discount at 15% a year and recompute LGD at the same point on the curve.

Part J

Expected Credit Loss

Now we put the three together, loan by loan:

ELi=PDi×LGD×EADi

PD is each loan’s own model PD (Part E), EAD is its outstanding balance (Part H), and LGD comes from the recovery curve (Part I), one LGD for all loans.

J1Expected loss for the book

Expected loss for every loan, totalled for the book.

J2Where does the expected loss come from?

The book broken down by DPD bucket: share of loans, share of exposure and share of expected loss.

J3Highest PD is not the same as biggest loss

The five loans with the highest PD, and the five with the highest expected loss.

J4What if next year is a bad year?

Our expected loss assumed a normal year. Part I showed that in a bad year (the COVID cohort) recoveries fall and LGD rises. Recompute expected loss using the LGD of the worst quarter instead of the average.

Summary

StepWhat we computedHowOur book

Part D: Merging the small segments

Assigns every loan to one of 10 pools (late loans keep only two score bands) and gives each loan its pool’s default rate as pd_pooled.

Part D
def assign_pool(row):
    if row["dpd_bucket"] == "0 DPD":                       # on-time loans: keep 4 score bands
        return f"0 DPD | {row['score_band']}"
    band = "<700" if row["bureau_score"] < 700 else "700+"  # late loans: only 2 score bands
    return f"{row['dpd_bucket']} | {band}"

df["pool"] = df.apply(assign_pool, axis=1)

pools = df.groupby("pool")["default_12m"].agg(loans="count", defaults="sum", pd_pooled="mean")
pools = pools.sort_values("pd_pooled")
print(pools.assign(pd_pooled=(pools["pd_pooled"] * 100).round(1).astype(str) + "%"))

df["pd_pooled"] = df["pool"].map(pools["pd_pooled"])      # give every loan its pool's PD
print(f"\nAverage pd_pooled across all loans: {df['pd_pooled'].mean():.1%}")
print(f"Actual default rate:                {df['default_12m'].mean():.1%}")

Part E: Logistic regression PD

Scales score and FOIR, turns DPD bucket and employment type into 0/1 columns, fits the logistic regression and prints coefficients and odds ratios. Each loan gets its own pd_model.

Part E
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X = pd.DataFrame({
    "Bureau score (+50 points)":  (df["bureau_score"] - 720) / 50,
    "FOIR (+10 percentage pts)":  (df["foir"] - 0.40) / 0.10,
    "Self-employed (vs salaried)": (df["employment_type"] == "Self-employed").astype(int),
    "1-30 DPD (vs on time)":      (df["dpd_bucket"] == "1-30 DPD").astype(int),
    "31-60 DPD (vs on time)":     (df["dpd_bucket"] == "31-60 DPD").astype(int),
    "61-89 DPD (vs on time)":     (df["dpd_bucket"] == "61-89 DPD").astype(int),
})
y = df["default_12m"]

model = LogisticRegression(C=1e9, max_iter=1000).fit(X, y)   # C=1e9 means "no penalty" – a plain logistic regression

result = pd.DataFrame({"coefficient": model.coef_[0],
                       "odds_ratio":  np.exp(model.coef_[0])}, index=X.columns).round(2)
print(result)

df["pd_model"] = model.predict_proba(X)[:, 1]                # each loan's own PD
print(f"\nAverage pd_model across all loans: {df['pd_model'].mean():.1%}")

Part G: Turning PD into action

Flags loans with PD of 20% or more and builds the confusion matrix against actual defaults.

Part G
cutoff = 0.20                                             # lender's policy: watch list if PD >= 20%

df["watch_list"] = (df["pd_model"] >= cutoff).astype(int)
matrix = pd.crosstab(df["default_12m"].map({0: "Actual: did NOT default", 1: "Actual: DEFAULTED"}),
                     df["watch_list"].map({0: "Not on watch list", 1: "On watch list"}),
                     margins=True, margins_name="Total")
matrix = matrix.reindex(["Actual: did NOT default", "Actual: DEFAULTED", "Total"])
matrix.index.name = None; matrix.columns.name = None
print(f"Watch list at PD cut-off of {cutoff:.0%}\n")
print(matrix, "\n")

caught = ((df.watch_list == 1) & (df.default_12m == 1)).sum()
missed = ((df.watch_list == 0) & (df.default_12m == 1)).sum()
false_alarm = ((df.watch_list == 1) & (df.default_12m == 0)).sum()
print(f"Defaulters caught on the watch list : {caught} of {caught + missed}")
print(f"Defaulters NOT on the watch list    : {missed}")
print(f"Watch-list loans that did not default: {false_alarm}")

missed_on_time = ((df.watch_list == 0) & (df.default_12m == 1) & (df.dpd_bucket == "0 DPD")).sum()
print(f"\nOf the {missed} missed defaulters, {missed_on_time} were paying on time (0 DPD) on the snapshot date.")

caught_50 = ((df.pd_model >= 0.5) & (df.default_12m == 1)).sum()
print(f"\nFor comparison, a 50% cut-off would catch only {caught_50} of {caught + missed} defaulters.")
